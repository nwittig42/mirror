# Mirror can forecast AI leads, honestly wide

Yes, the model can be built, and no AI-visibility product found in this research ships one, but it will be a rough estimate with a range of roughly ten to twenty times between its low and high ends, and for a typical small local business today the honest answer is often "a handful of extra leads a year", not dozens a month. The chain is: local Google search demand for the business's buyer questions (bought from DataForSEO), times a published ratio that converts Google searches into AI prompts, times the gap between the business's measured mention rate and its best competitor's (the part Mirror already measures), times the extra site visits each mention produces, times visit-to-lead and lead-to-customer rates, times the owner's own customer value. Only two links have solid footing: Mirror's own scan data and one 2026 panel study showing that a mention lifts the chance of a site visit by about 2 to 3 percentage points within a week. Every other link is a vendor benchmark, a proxy borrowed from Google search or paid ads, or an assumption, and several have no published benchmark at all. That is workable if the tab shows a range instead of a single number, labels each input as measured, owner-supplied or assumed, and frames the result as the size of an opportunity under a stated condition and never as what Mirror's service will deliver. In the United States the forecast is an advertising claim the moment a prospect sees it, so the evidence for every default has to be on file before launch and a footer disclaimer will not rescue a confident headline. The way to earn trust over time is the thing no competitor does: save every forecast, compare it with what happened, and publish the hit rate.

A note on evidence quality before the detail. Much of the research behind this report comes from vendors who sell AI-search tools, and a good share of the figures were read from secondary write-ups, not the original studies. Where a number is a researcher's own construction from the cited sources, this report says so. Nothing here is legal advice; the legal section flags what a US advertising lawyer should review.

## Nobody ships this forecast, and only one link has hard evidence

Classic SEO forecasting tools all use the same chain: search volume times click rate at a target position times conversion rate times customer value, with scenarios on top ([SEO Sherpa](https://seosherpa.com/seo-forecasting/)). SEOmonitor, SE Ranking, seoClarity and BrightEdge each ship a version, and BrightEdge presents aggressive, medium and conservative scenarios with editable assumptions ([BrightEdge](https://www.brightedge.com/products/s3/opportunity-forecasting)). None of them publishes an error band or a check of past forecasts against results. Their shared weakness is a fixed click curve: published click rates for the top Google result run from about 40% in one meta-analysis down to 1.6% on keywords that show an AI Overview, where the rate fell from 7.3% to 1.6% in two years ([OuterBox](https://outerboxdesign.com/articles/seo/ctr-based-on-organic-position/), [SEOworth summary of Ahrefs](https://seoworth.it.com/news/ahrefs-ai-overviews-ctr-study/)). The lesson for Mirror is to copy the structure (per-question inputs, named scenarios, editable rates, the formula shown line by line) and avoid hiding which rates were used.

Among AI-visibility products, **no forecast or opportunity-size figure was found** in Profound, AthenaHQ, Peec, Otterly, the Semrush AI toolkit, Ahrefs Brand Radar or Yext Scout. What exists is backward-looking attribution through Google Analytics, Shopify or Stripe. The one forward-looking tool found is a free calculator from Rank Prompt that starts from the business's own site traffic and an arbitrary "opportunity captured" slider, and describes itself as "a directional estimate, not a financial forecast" ([Rank Prompt](https://rankprompt.com/tools/free-geo-roi-calculator/)). This was a search of public pages, so the accurate statement is "none found", not "none exist". Mirror's advantage over that calculator is real: the gap comes from measured mention rates per competitor instead of a slider.

The evidence behind the chain is uneven, and the forecast should say so. The strongest link is that an AI mention raises the chance of a visit. Profound's July 2026 study followed more than 2 million AI conversations from a US opt-in panel and compared each person's visits in the week after seeing a brand named with that person's own baseline. **The lift was +2.07 points for ChatGPT, +2.96 for Google AI Overviews and +3.21 for Gemini**, each with a 95% confidence interval ([Profound](https://www.tryprofound.com/blog/the-ai-mention-effect)). Similarweb separately reported that brands recommended by ChatGPT were 2.5 times more likely to be visited within seven days ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/ai-recommended-brands-saw-2-5x-more-site-visits-similarweb/580241)). Both studies cover large national brands in finance, retail, travel, software and telecom, measure visits and not purchases, and are not randomised. Applying them to a dentist or a roofer is an extrapolation.

The weakest link is the one a business owner cares about most. **No study was found that connects a brand's share of AI mentions to its customers, revenue or market share**, for any size of business. The rule vendors borrow, from Binet and Field's advertising research, comes from ad spend, and even there excess share of voice explained only 6% to 12% of yearly market-share growth ([Selfstorming summary of Media in Focus](https://www.selfstorming.com/tools/libraries/wisdom/the-share-of-annual-market-share-growth-explained-by-excess-share)). A forecast that maps a visibility gap straight onto an equal customer gap would overstate badly. The model below therefore goes mention by mention through visits, and never assumes that matching a competitor's visibility means matching its customers.

## Demand comes from Google volume times a ratio, never from "AI search volume"

The first step is sizing how many AI answers get generated for the business's buyer questions in its area. No AI platform except Microsoft Copilot shares real query data, so every "AI search volume" figure on the market is a small panel scaled up, Google volume times a ratio, or a model built from Google's "People Also Ask" data ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-ahrefs-estimates-ai-search-demand/)). Panels cover "far less than 1%" of prompts, lean toward desktop and paid opt-in users, and get multiplied a hundredfold or more ([Conductor](https://www.conductor.com/academy/debunking-ai-prompt-volume/), [Scrunch](https://helpcenter.scrunchai.com/en/articles/13146531-understanding-prompt-volume-in-ai-search)). Nobody publishes an error range, and no measured prompt data exists at city or metro level from any source reviewed.

The most defensible approach for a small team is the transparent one. Take local Google search volume for the business's keyword set from DataForSEO's Google Ads endpoint, which costs about **$0.09 per request of up to 1,000 keywords** ([Ryan Doser review, August 2026](https://ryandoser.com/dataforseo-review/)), and multiply by Ahrefs' published platform ratios. As of September 2026 those are **ChatGPT 0.115, Gemini 0.011, Perplexity 0.01, Copilot 0.0033 and Grok 0.000054** prompts per Google search on the same topic ([Ahrefs help center](https://help.ahrefs.com/en/articles/16755865-what-is-ai-adjusted-volume-and-how-is-it-calculated)). Added together, the standalone assistants come to about 14 prompts per 100 Google searches. Claude is not in the table. The honest range comes from a definitional problem: Ahrefs' own analysis puts ChatGPT at 4% of Google's volume if only pure information-seeking prompts count, about 12% on the author's broader definition, and 18% if every prompt counts ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/chatgpt-has-12-percent-of-googles-search-volume/)). Peec independently lands on 4% to 12% ([Peec](https://docs.peec.ai/research/chatgpt-google-market-share)).

Three warnings belong on the explanation page. First, Ahrefs itself says this metric is directional and "not suitable for estimating AI-only addressable market size" ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/how-ahrefs-estimates-ai-search-demand/)); Mirror would be using it for roughly that purpose because nothing better exists. Second, the ratios are global and all-topic. No ratio by industry or by city was found, so the model assumes people ask AI about plumbers at the same rate as about everything else. Survey data hints that this is too generous for urgent trades: 45% of US consumers used AI for local business recommendations in the past year, against 22% of homeowners for home services ([BrightLocal](https://www.brightlocal.com/research/local-consumer-review-survey/), [Scorpion](https://www.scorpion.co/about-us/scorpion-news/scorpion-news/2026/scorpions-new-industry-report-reveals-a-disconne/)). Third, the ratios are moving. ChatGPT's weekly users roughly doubled in the year to early 2026 ([getairefs](https://getairefs.com/blog/chatgpt-user-statistics/)), so each ratio needs an "as of" date and a refresh at least twice a year.

DataForSEO's own `ai_search_volume` field is cheap at about $0.0001 per keyword, but its documentation says it is "calculated using statistical data from questions in the 'People Also Ask' SERP element" ([DataForSEO docs](https://docs.dataforseo.com/v3/ai_optimization/ai_keyword_data/keywords_search_volume/live.md)). It is Google data relabelled, not a count of prompts, and it is not an independent check on the Ahrefs method because both rest on Google demand. Use it at most as a national cross-check, and call the top-of-funnel number "modelled from Google search demand" everywhere.

Google AI Overviews are the one place where measurement beats benchmarks. Published trigger rates for local queries disagree wildly: 15% in a 540-query hand study, 21.8% in a 2026 vendor report, 3% for restaurant queries, and 76.9% of "near me" queries in Seer's client set ([Whitespark](https://whitespark.ca/blog/case-study-the-prevalence-of-ai-overviews-in-local-search/), [Hartzer](https://hartzer.it.com/steady-demand-local-citations-2026/), [cloro](https://cloro.dev/research/ai-overview-trigger-index/), [Seer Interactive](https://www.seerinteractive.com/insights/aio-impact-on-google-ctr-2026-update)). Mirror already buys DataForSEO search results at $0.0006 to $0.002 per page, so it can count how often an AI Overview appears on each client's actual keywords in their actual city. That measured rate replaces any benchmark.

Two tempting data sources should stay out of the number. Competitor website traffic is cheap from DataForSEO (about $0.012 per request plus $0.00012 per domain) but it is built as rank times volume times click rate, counts only Google organic visits, and has never been tested for accuracy on small sites ([DataForSEO docs](https://docs.dataforseo.com/v3/dataforseo_labs-google-bulk_traffic_estimation-live/)). The tools that have been tested were "at least 40% incorrect" on nearly every site under 10,000 visits and overshot by six to ten times on a new site ([Screaming Frog](https://www.screamingfrog.co.uk/how-accurate-are-website-traffic-estimators/), [Geotoolbox](https://geotoolbox.ai/blog/semrush-vs-ahrefs.md)). Competitor review counts are also cheap, at $0.00075 per 10 reviews ([DataForSEO pricing](https://dataforseo.com/pricing/business-data/google-reviews-api)), but the only reviews-per-customer figure found is one agency's self-reported 10 per 100 customers asked, with individual campaigns running from 7.8% to 74.8% ([Relentless Digital](https://www.relentless-digital.com/google-reviews-per-100-past-customers)). Both are useful as a sanity check on which competitor is bigger, and as a plausibility cap on the forecast. Neither is good enough to print as a customer count.

## From a mention to a customer, the benchmarks thin out fast

Once the model knows how many more answers would name the business, it needs the share of those that become visits. There are two ways to estimate this, and they roughly agree. The direct route uses Profound's measured lift of about 2 to 3 extra visits per 100 exposures. The indirect route starts from clicks: Pew found users clicked a link inside a Google AI summary in **1% of visits** ([Pew Research](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2025/07/22/google-users-are-less-likely-to-click-on-links-when-an-ai-summary-appears-in-the-results/)), and Similarweb found 55.9% of AI-influenced visits arrive later through a search and not a visible AI referral ([Search Engine Land](https://searchengineland.com/chatgpt-recommendations-brand-website-visits-study-480989)), which the researchers turned into a multiplier of roughly two to three on tracked clicks. One percent times three is about 3 visits per 100 exposures. Two routes built on different data landing in the same place is modest reassurance, and it is this report's own comparison, not a published finding.

The model should use the Profound lift as its visit step because it is the only figure with a confidence interval and it already includes visits that arrive without an AI referral tag. That matters because click tracking sees very little. Only 6.8% of ChatGPT conversations contained any web citation in May 2026 ([Similarweb](https://aisearch.similarweb.com/blog/ai-referral-traffic-by-industry/)), and Profound found more than 97% of the resulting brand-site visits carried no tracking parameter. For small local businesses the range should be widened below Profound's interval, because a little-known business has less name recall and local buyers often phone or open Maps instead of visiting a site. **No study measures calls, map taps or bookings that start from an AI answer**, and no study measures how position within an answer (named first, one of five, cited as a source only) changes the outcome.

For visit-to-lead and lead-to-customer rates, the best default is the business's own numbers. Where those are missing, the researchers built ranges by business type from three sources: LocaliQ's 2026 paid-search benchmarks, where an ad click becomes a lead 8.18% of the time across industries and 5.55% to 16.22% for local categories ([LocaliQ](https://localiq.com/blog/search-advertising-benchmarks/)); First Page Sage's site-wide visitor-to-lead rates of 1.1% to 3.1% for most industries and 7.4% for legal ([First Page Sage](https://firstpagesage.com/reports/b2b-conversion-rates-by-industry-fc/)); and Invoca's finding that about 42% of phone leads convert on the call ([Invoca](https://www.invoca.com/blog/lead-conversion-rate-benchmarks)). Paid-ad rates are an upper bound because those clicks land on purpose-built pages with buying intent. The resulting ranges are in the recommended model below. They are constructions, not published benchmarks, and the lead-to-customer figures for dental, clinics, legal and restaurants rest on thin ground.

Whether AI-referred visitors convert better than ordinary visitors is contested. First Page Sage's 218-site client panel shows ChatGPT visitors becoming leads at 4.7% against 1.9% for Google organic ([First Page Sage](https://firstpagesage.com/seo-blog/ai-impact-on-website-traffic-report/)), while the largest e-commerce dataset, 973 sites published in Marketing Science, found ChatGPT converting about 13% worse than organic through mid-2025 ([INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/full/10.1287/mksc.2025.0489)). Single-site claims of 16% or 23-times conversion are anecdotes from software companies. The defensible default is no uplift: start AI-driven visits at the business's ordinary site-wide rate and let the upper end of the range carry the possibility that they convert better.

Customer value has no usable benchmark. Every figure found for dental, med spa or HVAC lifetime value came from a vendor blog with no stated method. The model should ask the owner for an average sale or first-year value and show revenue only when they supply it. A sourced fallback exists for owners who will not: value each lead at what businesses already pay Google for one, which LocaliQ puts at **$131.63 for legal, $90.92 for home improvement, $72.97 for dental and $30.57 for restaurants** ([LocaliQ](https://localiq.com/blog/search-advertising-benchmarks/)). "These leads would cost about this much to buy as ads" is a claim Mirror can back up.

Mirror's accuracy score deserves a specific word. No research was found on how much a wrong AI answer about a business (wrong hours, prices, services) costs it in leads. The accuracy score should appear beside the forecast as a plain-language warning that some mentions are being wasted, and should not be multiplied into the number until Mirror has its own data.

## Combine the ranges properly and show every step

A funnel of six uncertain numbers needs care in how the ranges are combined. Multiplying every low together and every high together is wrong: if each of six steps has a high three times its low, that method gives a spread of 729 times, while the chance of all six sitting at their low end together is about 1 in 64 million. Treating the steps as independent, the spreads add as a square root in log terms and the real spread is about 15 times. This arithmetic is the researchers' own derivation from standard statistics. In practice Mirror should run a small simulation, 10,000 random draws in TypeScript, with every rate capped below 100%, and report the median with the middle 80% of outcomes. The median matters because a product of uncertain numbers is skewed, and the average sits well above what a typical business gets. People who set ranges by judgment are overconfident, with true values landing inside "90%" ranges only about half the time in one cited study ([summary of Hubbard, How to Measure Anything](https://www.greaterwrong.com/posts/ybYBCK9D7MZCcdArB/how-to-measure-anything)), so first-version ranges should be wider than feels comfortable.

Showing a range does not cost trust. A 2020 study across five UK surveys with 5,780 people found that giving a numeric range made people slightly less certain of the exact figure but did not reduce trust in the source, and worked better than a vague verbal caveat ([Winton Centre, University of Cambridge](https://wintoncentre.maths.cam.ac.uk/news/being-upfront-about-your-uncertainty-doesnt-necessarily-mean-you-lose-your-audiences-trust-new-paper-published)). That study tested news facts, not sales forecasts from a company with something to sell, so the range should be paired with a published track record once one exists. The practical rules: lead with one likely number and put the range beside it at similar size, use numbers and never just "results may vary", and round hard to whole leads and customers.

The "how we got this number" page should follow the strongest public example, Zillow, which names its figure an estimate, publishes its error rate, splits that rate by data quality (roughly 2% median error for homes on the market against roughly 7% off-market) and refreshes it regularly ([The Close](https://theclose.com/how-accurate-is-zillow-zestimate/)). Google's Project Sunroof shows the working on the same screen as the number and lets the user type in their own electricity bill ([Boston Globe](https://www.bostonglobe.com/business/2015/08/17/see-what-you-can-save-with-google-solar-map/CvAOl0p1lrspy2YXuaTqIL/story.html)). Sunroof also carries the warning most relevant to Mirror: an independent review found its physical measurement sound and its savings figure weak ([MREA](https://midwestrenew.org/comparing-sunroof/)). Mirror has the same shape. The scan is solid and the money conversion on top of it is not, so the page should present them as two visibly separate layers.

| Part of the page | What it shows |
|---|---|
| What this is | One sentence: an estimate of an opportunity under a stated condition, not a promise or a guarantee |
| The working | The funnel as one sentence, then a table with each step, the value used, its low and high, and its source and date |
| Where each input came from | Every row labelled "measured by Mirror", "bought data", "your number" or "industry assumption" |
| Change the inputs | The owner can edit visit-to-lead rate, close rate and customer value and watch the number move |
| What moves it most | The two or three inputs that swing the result |
| What is not counted | Phone calls and map taps straight from an AI answer, Google AI Mode, growth in AI use, repeat business |
| Track record | Once it exists: "in X of 100 past forecasts the real result landed inside our range", split by customers with connected data versus defaults only |
| Version | Date last updated and a change log, because benchmarks move |

The words matter as much as the layout. HubSpot's ROI calculator has shown "Typical +18%" in one version and "Average +164%" in another ([HubSpot](https://www.hubspot.com/roi-calculator)), which is the difference between what most customers get and a figure dragged up by a few winners. Mirror should say "likely" and mean the median.

## US law treats the forecast as an advertising claim

The governing standard is Section 5 of the FTC Act and the substantiation doctrine. An ad is deceptive if it is likely to mislead a reasonable buyer on something that matters, and "advertisers must have proof to back up express and implied claims" before the ad runs ([FTC, Advertising FAQs](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business)). A "reasonable basis" means objective evidence, held in advance ([FTC Policy Statement on Advertising Substantiation](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/ftc-policy-statement-regarding-advertising-substantiation)). The forecast shown in a free audit is marketing. The express claim is "likely about N more customers". The implied claim, which is the dangerous one, is "pay Mirror and you will get about N more customers". The model estimates what better visibility could produce. It says nothing about what Mirror's service will achieve, and the page must say that in plain words next to the number.

Disclaimers do not fix a misleading headline. The FTC says advertisers cannot "use fine print to contradict other statements in an ad or to clear up misimpressions", and disclosures must be clear and conspicuous ([FTC, Advertising FAQs](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/advertising-faqs-guide-small-business)). A footer "results not guaranteed" under a large confident number fails this test. What works is building the qualification into the claim: the word "estimate", the range, and the condition ("if AI tools named you as often as your top competitor") all on the same screen as the number. Stating a confidence level is itself a claim, since the FTC expects a firm to hold "at least the advertised level of substantiation". Until forecasts have been checked against results, Mirror should write "our estimated range" and never print an accuracy percentage or the word "proven".

Selling to businesses is no shelter. In January 2023 the FTC ordered HomeAdvisor to pay up to $7.2 million over deceptive marketing of leads sold to home-service providers ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2023/01/ftc-order-requires-homeadvisor-pay-72-million-stop-deceptively-marketing-its-leads-home-improvement)). In August 2025 it finalised an order against Workado, which had advertised an AI detector as 98% accurate when testing showed about 53%; the order bars accuracy claims unless backed by competent and reliable evidence held at the time ([Frankfurt Kurnit](https://advertisinglaw.fkks.com/post/102l358/ftcs-ai-crackdown-continues-workado-ordered-to-drop-unsupported-accuracy-claims)). No FTC case was found against an SEO or marketing agency specifically for inflated forecasts, which is an absence in this search and not a clearance. Competitors can also sue over ad claims under the Lanham Act, and California's Business and Professions Code section 17500 prohibits statements the speaker knew or should have known were misleading ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-7/part-3/chapter-1/article-1/section-17500)). Only California was checked among the states.

The FTC's Business Opportunity Rule and its proposed earnings-claim rules target people selling ways to make money, and they probably do not reach a measurement service sold to an existing business. The January 2025 proposal to extend the rule to "money-making opportunities" such as coaching is the one to watch ([Hunton](https://hunton.com/hunton-retail-law-resource/ftc-issues-three-notices-for-changes-to-the-business-opportunity-rule-and-a-new-rule-on-multi-level-marketing-mlm-practices)). As of September 2026 it was reported as proposed and not final, though that status rests on a single unofficial tracker ([MMOL Database](https://mmoldatabase.com/the-rules.html)) and should be confirmed.

Several practical limits follow. Keep a substantiation file before launch with each default's source, date and reason it applies. Do not place a best-customer story beside a prospect's forecast, because the Endorsement Guides require typical results to be made clear ([FTC Endorsement Guides FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/ftcs-endorsement-guides-what-people-are-asking)). Avoid "guaranteed", "will", "up to", and "you are losing $X to competitors" stated as fact. Do not set the forecast against Mirror's price as a payback multiple in the free audit. A lawyer should review the headline wording, the confidence language, whether revenue appears in the free audit at all, the terms-of-service clause on estimates, and the business-opportunity question.

## A published hit rate is the only way to tighten the range

No backtest of any SEO or AI-visibility forecast was found from a vendor, agency or academic. The nearest audited field, road-traffic forecasting, shows typical errors of 17% to 40% with a lean toward over-prediction ([Virginia Transportation Research Council](https://vtrc.virginia.gov/reports/all-reports/17-r1/), [University of Kentucky](https://uknowledge.uky.edu/ce_etds/79)). Mirror should expect to be further off than that at first, and expect to be too high more often than too low. Logging forecasts and scoring them later would be a real point of difference.

Checking requires measuring AI-driven leads, and no single method sees most of them. Google Analytics files a visit with no referrer as Direct, clicks from AI Overviews count as ordinary Google organic, and vendor estimates of the share of AI visits arriving unlabelled run from 35% to 71% ([AuthorityTech](https://authoritytech.io/curated/ai-search-traffic-invisible-attribution-analytics-measurement-2026)). CallRail attributes only about 0.1% of inbound calls to AI search ([CallRail via MarTech Cube](https://www.martechcube.com/?p=287309)). Yet when the software company Docebo made "How did you hear about us?" a required field, 12.7% of high-intent leads named an AI tool ([Growth Unhinged](https://kylepoyar.substack.com/p/ai-discovery-playbook)). Those figures come from different companies and cannot be compared directly, but the direction is clear: asking the customer is the most informative cheap signal. The measurement kit for a small team is a required "How did you hear about us?" question with an AI option on every form and phone intake, a Google Analytics channel group that catches referrers such as chatgpt.com and perplexity.ai, and trend lines for branded search and Google Business Profile calls and clicks shown beside Mirror's mention rate as supporting evidence.

The forecast should be scored against what can be counted: leads that self-report an AI source plus leads from the tracked AI channel. That count is a floor on the truth, so the page should say that tracking sees only part of the effect, without quoting a precise "we miss X%" because no independent source supports one.

Percentage error is the wrong score when a business gets zero to five such leads a month, since it is undefined at zero and explodes near it ([Forecasting: Principles and Practice](https://otexts.com/fpp3/accuracy.html)). Three scores fit better. Coverage is the share of forecasts where the real result fell inside the stated range, which should match the stated level of about 8 in 10. Bias is the share of forecasts that were too high versus too low, which should sit near half each. Median absolute error is in whole leads. If coverage comes in low, widen the ranges before moving the midpoint.

Recalibration itself is one line of arithmetic. Blend each default rate with the customer's own observed rate, weighted by how much data they have: blended rate = (default rate × k + customer's successes) ÷ (k + customer's trials), where k is how many observations' worth of trust the default gets ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/empirical-bayes/)). With a 3% default lead rate, k of 100, and a customer who got 4 leads from 50 AI visits, the blended rate is 4.7%. In plain words for the owner: "We start from the industry average and move toward your numbers as we see more of them." Every quarter, re-estimate the industry defaults from all customers, tune on older periods and score on later ones, version the model, and publish the scorecard including the bad results. Two cautions belong on the scorecard: free-audit forecasts cannot be scored unless the prospect becomes a customer, and customers who stay long enough to be scored are probably the ones doing better, so report how many forecasts were scored out of how many were issued. The 90-day scoring window and quarterly cadence are judgment calls, not sourced figures.

## The recommended model, step by step

The forecast answers one conditional question: "If AI tools named you as often as they name your most-recommended competitor, about how many more leads and customers would that be, at today's level of AI use?" It runs per business, per month, and is shown per year when the monthly figure rounds to zero.

| Step | What it is | How it is calculated | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Local Google searches per month for the business's buyer questions | DataForSEO Google Ads search volume for the keyword set behind the scanned questions, in the business's service area | Bought data |
| 2a | Assistant answers per month (ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude, Grok) | Step 1 × assistant ratio | Assumed, published ratio |
| 2b | Google AI Overview answers per month | Step 1 × share of the business's own keywords that show an AI Overview in DataForSEO search results | Measured |
| 3 | Mention-rate gap | Best competitor's mention rate minus the business's, from Mirror scans, weighted toward ChatGPT and AI Overviews | Measured by Mirror |
| 4 | Extra answers naming the business | (Step 2a + Step 2b) × Step 3 | Calculated |
| 5 | Extra visits | Step 4 × visit lift per mention | Assumed, one strong study |
| 6 | Extra leads | Step 5 × visit-to-lead rate | Owner's number, else industry assumption |
| 7 | Extra customers | Step 6 × lead-to-customer rate | Owner's number, else industry assumption |
| 8 | Value | Step 7 × owner's average customer value; if not supplied, Step 6 × cost of an equivalent paid lead | Owner's number, or sourced fallback |

The ranges are combined by simulation, and the tab shows the median as "likely" with the middle 80% as "our estimated range". The defaults are below.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Assistant prompts per Google search, all assistants | 0.05 | 0.14 | 0.22 | Ahrefs ratio table summed to about 0.14 ([Ahrefs help center](https://help.ahrefs.com/en/articles/16755865-what-is-ai-adjusted-volume-and-how-is-it-calculated)); low and high scale Ahrefs' 4% and 18% ChatGPT bounds ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/chatgpt-has-12-percent-of-googles-search-volume/)). The visit-to-lead and lead-to-customer rows are the researchers' constructions from the cited sources, not published ranges. Any business type without a row, and restaurants in particular, where no close-rate source exists, should see "not enough data to estimate yet" until the owner supplies their own rates.

A worked illustration with invented inputs shows the scale to expect. Take a home-services business whose keyword set gets 10,000 local Google searches a month, with AI Overviews measured on 20% of them, a current mention rate of 10% and a top competitor at 30%. Steps 2a and 2b give roughly 3,400 AI answers a month (range 2,500 to 4,200). The 20-point gap is about 680 extra mentions. At 2 visits per 100, that is about 14 extra visits a month, then about half a lead a month at 4%, then roughly one extra customer a year at 25%. Combining the ranges properly gives **about 4 to 7 extra leads a year, with a plausible range of 1 to 16, and about one extra customer a year, with a range of zero to 4**. That is a small number, and it is the honest one at today's level of AI use. The result scales in a straight line with search demand and customer value, so the forecast will look meaningful for businesses with large search demand or high-value sales (legal, B2B, clinics, multi-location firms) and modest for a single-location trade.

Three guardrails complete the model. Before forecasting a gain, run the same chain on the business's current mention rate and compare the implied AI visits with what its analytics show; if they are far apart, say so and widen the range. Cap the result against a plausibility check, such as the business's own total monthly leads, so the forecast never implies AI visibility would double a business overnight. Require a minimum number of scanned buyer questions before showing any number.

Version one should leave out multi-year and lifetime revenue totals, projected growth in AI use (AI referral traffic is roughly doubling yearly, per [First Page Sage](https://firstpagesage.com/seo-blog/ai-impact-on-website-traffic-report/) and [Similarweb](https://aisearch.similarweb.com/blog/ai-referral-traffic-by-industry/), but compounding it inflates the headline), per-engine lead breakdowns, any accuracy percentage, and decimals.

## Conclusion

The research changes what the Forecast tab is for. It began as a way to put a customer number on Mirror's visibility score. The evidence supports something narrower and more durable: a transparent calculation that turns a measured gap into a small, wide, conditional estimate, and then gets checked. At today's AI usage the estimate will underwhelm many small local businesses, and showing that plainly is a strength, because the same chain will produce larger numbers as AI use grows and the track record will already exist to back them.

The scarce asset is the missing data, not the formula. Almost every gap in the table above (visit lift for small businesses, calls from AI answers, the cost of inaccurate answers, real conversion by trade) is something Mirror's own customers generate once a "How did you hear about us?" field and a saved forecast are in place. Within a year of logging, Mirror would hold the only small-business benchmarks in this field, and the forecast's defaults could cite Mirror's own measured results in place of vendor studies. Two cheap checks should happen before any build: one test call to see whether DataForSEO's volume endpoints return city-level figures for a real client's keywords, and a lawyer's read of the headline wording.

## Addendum: forecasting the Accuracy score (13 October 2026)

The Accuracy score is exact arithmetic (each wrong fact in the latest scan costs known points), so the only uncertain part of forecasting it is time: how long after a source is corrected AI stops repeating the mistake. **No measured rate exists.** No study times corrections, as opposed to newly planted facts, for any AI tool. The figures below are estimates from the nearest evidence, and Mirror's own before-and-after scans would be the first real dataset.

| Where AI read the wrong fact | Gone by 4 weeks | 8 weeks | 12 weeks | Anchored on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The business's own website | 35 / 50 / 60% | 50 / 65 / 75% | 60 / 75 / 85% | Search pickup in 1 day to 3 weeks in small tests; ChatGPT followed the official page in 84% of answers ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-vs-made-up-brand-experiment/)) |
| An editable listing (Google, Yelp, directories) | 20 / 35 / 45% | 40 / 55 / 65% | 50 / 65 / 75% | Data providers push in 1 day to 1 month, downstream sites weekly to yearly ([BrightLocal](https://help.brightlocal.com/hc/en-us/articles/360036410794)) |
| A third-party page that cannot be edited | 3 / 8 / 15% | 5 / 15 / 25% | 10 / 22 / 35% | Perplexity and Gemini repeated third-party claims in 37 to 39% of answers despite an official denial ([Ahrefs](https://ahrefs.com/blog/ai-vs-made-up-brand-experiment/)) |
| No source cited (model memory) | 2 / 5 / 10% | 3 / 8 / 15% | 5 / 15 / 25% | Training data ends 4 or more months before a model's release, so a fix cannot reach it within 12 weeks |

Other evidence: Full Fact followed 39 errors across Gemini, Grok and ChatGPT for five months and saw some corrected days after a fact-check was published and several never ([Full Fact](https://fullfact.org/technology/full-fact-analysis--ai-chatbots-misinformation/)). Insites found only 55.6% of ChatGPT answers and 48.1% of Perplexity answers fully matched the Google Business Profile across 10,000 US businesses ([Insites](https://insites.com/blog/does-chatgpt-hallucinate-local-businesses-the-data-on-ai-accuracy/)). Estimates of the share of ChatGPT prompts that use live search run from about 20% to 60% across vendors, which is why the own-site ceiling is below 100%.
